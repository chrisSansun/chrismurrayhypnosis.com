Hypnotherapy for Chronic Pain: How It Works and What the Research Shows

Key Takeaways

  • Pain is produced by the brain and nervous system, not only by tissue damage. That is why attention, emotion, and expectation change how much it hurts.
  • Research reviews, including by Patterson and Jensen, suggest hypnosis can reduce chronic pain intensity for many people.
  • Hypnotherapy is a complement to medical care, not a way to mask an undiagnosed problem.
  • NICE guidance for chronic primary pain emphasises exercise, psychological therapies, and some medication. Hypnosis is not a core recommendation.
  • Get new or changing pain assessed by a doctor before starting any pain-management approach.

Living with pain that does not go away wears down everything: sleep, mood, work, relationships, and your sense of who you are. And if scans are clear and treatments have only helped a little, it can feel as though no one quite believes you.

Your pain is real. Understanding how the brain contributes to pain does not make it imaginary. It opens another set of options.

How the Brain Shapes Pain

Pain is not a simple signal from damaged tissue to the brain. The brain constructs the experience of pain from sensory information, context, emotion, attention, and prior experience. That is why the same injury can hurt very differently depending on circumstances.

In chronic pain, the nervous system can become sensitised, so that it amplifies signals and reacts to things that should not hurt. Stress, poor sleep, fear of movement, and low mood all feed this. Understanding that the system is overprotective, not that something is still being damaged, can change how you relate to it.

What the Research Shows

Hypnosis has a longer research record for pain than for most other conditions. Reviews by Patterson and Jensen in the early 2000s, and a later review by Jensen and Patterson, concluded that hypnotic interventions can produce significant reductions in chronic pain for many people, with benefits that vary between individuals.

Earlier meta-analytic work by Montgomery and colleagues found that a majority of people achieved meaningful pain relief from hypnotic analgesia in experimental settings. Results differ across conditions and studies, and not everyone responds.

To keep this in proportion: NICE guidance for chronic primary pain (NG193) focuses on exercise, certain psychological therapies, and some medication, and does not list hypnosis as a core recommendation. The evidence supports hypnosis as a useful tool for some people rather than a standard treatment for all.

What We Do in Sessions

We use hypnotic techniques to change your relationship with pain. That can involve altering where attention goes, changing the sensory quality of the pain in imagination, building a sense of control, and reducing the fear and catastrophising that amplify it.

You also learn self-hypnosis, which is often the most valuable part. Pain does not keep office hours, so having a method you can use at home matters more than anything that happens in the room.

Sleep, mood, and pacing of activity are part of the work too, because they all feed into pain. See self-hypnosis for more on how that practice works.

Important: Any new, worsening, or unexplained pain should be assessed by a doctor first. Hypnotherapy must never be used to override a signal that needs medical attention.

If pain has become the centre of your life, learning to change how your nervous system handles it may be worth a conversation.

What to Expect From the First Few Sessions

We begin with a thorough conversation about your pain: where it is, what makes it better or worse, how it affects sleep, mood, and activity, and what you have already tried. I will also ask about your medical care and want to know that new symptoms have been properly assessed.

Early sessions introduce the hypnotic state and start to build a sense of control. Over time we work on the quality of the pain, the attention you give it, and the beliefs and fears around it, while you practise self-hypnosis at home.

Common Myths About Hypnosis and Pain

One myth is that hypnosis simply tricks you into not noticing pain. In fact, you remain aware throughout, and what changes is how your nervous system processes and responds to the signal.

Another is that if hypnosis helps, the pain must have been psychological. It does not follow. Attention, expectation, and emotion influence all pain, including pain with a clear physical cause. A method that works through those channels is not evidence that the pain was imagined.

Working With Your Medical Care

I work alongside your GP, pain specialist, and physiotherapist, not in place of them. If you are on pain medication, keep taking it as prescribed. Any changes should be agreed with your prescriber.

Many people find hypnosis lets them do more with the same level of pain, sleep better, and feel less defined by it. That is a meaningful outcome even when the pain does not disappear entirely.

Who It Tends to Suit

It tends to suit people who are willing to practise, who have already had a medical assessment, and who want an approach that gives them some control. It may be less effective if you are looking for something to be done to you without active participation.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can hypnosis really reduce chronic pain?

Research reviews suggest it can reduce pain intensity for many people, though responses vary. It is best understood as one tool alongside medical care.

Is the pain all in my head?

No. Pain is real. The brain is involved in producing every pain experience, which is why psychological and attentional approaches can change it.

Do I need a diagnosis first?

Yes. Any new or changing pain should be assessed by a doctor before you start hypnotherapy.

Can I use self-hypnosis at home?

Yes. Learning self-hypnosis is a core part of the work, and a personalised recording supports your practice.

Will it replace my medication?

No. Continue medication as prescribed, and discuss any changes with your prescriber.
