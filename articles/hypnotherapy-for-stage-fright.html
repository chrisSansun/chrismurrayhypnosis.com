Hypnotherapy for Stage Fright: Performing Without the Dread

Key Takeaways

  • Stage fright is common among professional performers. A systematic review found music performance anxiety in between 16.5% and 60% of professional musicians, depending on the study.
  • Nerves are not the enemy. Research suggests that reframing arousal as excitement can work better than trying to calm down.
  • The trouble starts when the body's alarm is linked to a fear of judgement, exposure or failure, and then to anticipation weeks in advance.
  • Hypnotherapy works on that link, rehearses the performance in a calm and focused state, and builds a reliable pre-performance routine.
  • Research on treatments for performance anxiety, including hypnotherapy, is still at an early stage. Severe or spreading anxiety should be discussed with your GP.

You have done this hundreds of times. You know the material, the piece, the lines. And still, as the moment approaches, your hands shake, your mouth goes dry, and a voice in your head insists that this will be the time it all goes wrong.

Stage fright is not a beginner's problem. Some of the most accomplished performers I work with have it, and many have hidden it for years. It can affect musicians, actors, dancers, speakers, and anyone else whose work involves being watched.

What Stage Fright Is

Stage fright is a form of performance anxiety: fear before or during a performance that is strong enough to be distressing or to affect how you perform. It usually has three parts. There is the physical alarm, such as a racing heart, trembling, nausea or a tight throat. There are the thoughts, which predict failure and imagine the audience's judgement. And there is the behaviour, such as over-practising, avoiding opportunities, or relying on rituals to get through.

It is far more common than most performers realise. A systematic review by Fernholz and colleagues found that the prevalence of music performance anxiety among professional musicians ranged from 16.5% to 60% across studies. More women than men were affected, and musicians over about 45 to 50 reported less performance anxiety than younger musicians.

If your fear is mainly about speaking at work, see hypnotherapy for public speaking. This article focuses on performance in the wider sense.

Why Experience Does Not Fix It

People assume stage fright fades with practice. Sometimes it does. Often it does not, and occasionally it gets worse as a career develops, because the stakes rise. A bigger venue, a more important audition or a more demanding critic gives the fear more to work with.

Underneath it there is usually a belief that a performance is a verdict on you as a person, not just an event. If one mistake would prove you are a fraud, every mistake becomes a threat. That belief links stage fright closely to perfectionism and imposter feelings.

There is also anticipation. For many performers the worst part is not the performance itself but the days or weeks before it, when the mind rehearses disaster over and over. That rehearsal trains the fear.

Nerves Are Not the Enemy

Most performers try to calm down. That is understandable, but it may not be the best strategy. In a series of experiments involving singing, public speaking and maths tasks, Alison Wood Brooks found that people who reappraised their anxious arousal as excitement, for example by saying "I am excited" out loud, felt more excited and performed better than people who tried to calm down.

The reason seems to be that anxiety and excitement feel physically similar. Shifting from high arousal to calm is a big jump. Shifting from one high-arousal state to another is easier, and it moves you from a threat mindset to an opportunity mindset.

This matters because the goal is not to perform with no adrenaline at all. Most good performers need some energy. The goal is to change what that energy means to you.

If the dread before a performance has started to outweigh the joy of doing it, that is worth a conversation.

What Cognitive Hypnotherapy Does

We begin by mapping your particular pattern. When does the fear start? What does your body do, and in what order? What is the worst thing you imagine happening, and what would it mean about you? Stage fright is rarely random. It follows a sequence, and that sequence gives us a way in.

In the hypnotic work, we do several things. We work on the belief that turns a performance into a verdict, and on any earlier experiences that gave it force, such as a public humiliation or a harsh teacher. We rehearse the performance in a calm, focused state, walking through the build-up, the walk on, the opening moments and a recovery from a small mistake, so that your nervous system gets repeated experience of performing without alarm. And we practise reframing arousal, so that a racing heart becomes a signal of readiness rather than danger.

We also build a short pre-performance routine that you can use backstage: a breath, an anchor, a phrase, a focus point. Rehearsed many times in hypnosis and practice, it becomes something you can rely on when the pressure is high. You leave with a personalised recording to use in the run-up to performances.

Where the Evidence Stands

I want to be honest about the research. Fernholz and colleagues found that CBT and beta blockers were the most researched treatments for performance anxiety in professional musicians, with beneficial results for CBT, but noted that better-controlled studies were needed. A 2025 systematic review by Kinney and colleagues looked at 40 studies of interventions including CBT, acceptance and commitment therapy, mindfulness, virtual reality, biofeedback and hypnotherapy. Most showed encouraging improvements, but the authors concluded the evidence base was still in its infancy and that firm recommendations would be premature.

So hypnotherapy is one reasonable option among several, not a proven cure. It fits well with the cognitive principles that have the most support, such as working on beliefs and rehearsing new responses.

Limits and When to Seek Other Help

Hypnotherapy is not a substitute for medical or psychiatric care. If your anxiety has spread beyond performance into everyday social situations, you may be dealing with social anxiety, for which NICE recommends individual CBT. If you experience panic attacks, very low mood, or you are relying on alcohol or other substances to get through performances, please speak to your GP. Some performers are prescribed propranolol, which the NHS says can help with symptoms of anxiety. That is a decision for you and your doctor, and hypnotherapy can be used alongside it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can hypnotherapy cure stage fright?

No honest practitioner should promise a cure. A recent systematic review found encouraging results across several approaches, including hypnotherapy, but said the evidence was too limited for firm recommendations. Many performers find hypnotherapy helps them manage the fear and rehearse calmly.

Will I lose my edge if I stop being nervous?

The aim is not to remove all arousal. Some activation helps performance. The aim is to change how you interpret and respond to it, so that energy works for you rather than against you.

What about beta blockers?

Some performers are prescribed propranolol, a beta blocker that the NHS says can help with symptoms of anxiety. It is a prescription medicine and must be discussed with your doctor. Hypnotherapy can be used alongside it.

How far in advance should I start?

Ideally several weeks before an important performance, so there is time to work on the pattern and practise with your recording. Short-notice sessions can still help with a pre-performance routine.

Can I do this online?

Yes. Online sessions work well, and you can practise in the room where you rehearse. See how online hypnotherapy works.
