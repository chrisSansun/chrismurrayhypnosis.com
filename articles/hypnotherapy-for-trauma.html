Hypnotherapy for Trauma and PTSD: Safety, Evidence, and Limits

Key Takeaways

  • NICE recommends trauma-focused CBT and EMDR as first-line treatment for PTSD. Hypnotherapy is not a substitute.
  • Trauma work is best done in stages. Stabilisation and safety come before processing painful memories.
  • Hypnosis can support stabilisation: calming the body, building a sense of safety, and reducing nightmares and hyperarousal.
  • Hypnosis is not a reliable way to recover memories, and I do not use it to try to uncover them.
  • If you have been through severe trauma, work with a trauma-trained clinician. Hypnotherapy can complement that work.

Trauma is not only what happened to you. It is what stayed in you: the startle, the flashbacks, the numbness, the sense that the danger has not ended even though it has. Many capable, successful people live this way for years.

Because the stakes here are high, I want to be unusually clear about what I can and cannot offer, and how hypnotherapy relates to the established treatments.

What Trauma Does to Mind and Body

After an overwhelming experience, the brain's threat system can stay on high alert. Memory is stored differently, so reminders trigger body reactions and flashbacks as if the event were happening now. Sleep suffers, concentration drops, and many people either feel flooded or go numb.

The NHS describes PTSD as a condition that can follow experiencing or witnessing a frightening, distressing event, with symptoms such as re-experiencing, avoidance, and heightened arousal. Not everyone with trauma reactions has PTSD, but the underlying mechanisms overlap.

What Is Recommended First

NICE guidance on PTSD (NG116) recommends trauma-focused psychological therapies, specifically trauma-focused CBT and EMDR, as first-line treatment. These are structured approaches delivered by clinicians trained to work with traumatic memory safely.

I would always encourage anyone with significant PTSD to work with a trauma-trained therapist. Hypnotherapy is something that can sit alongside that work, not replace it.

Why Stage Matters

A widely used framework, set out by Judith Herman in her book on trauma and recovery, describes three stages: safety and stabilisation, processing of the traumatic memory, and reconnection with life. Rushing into the second stage before the first is secure can make people worse.

This is where I am cautious. Hypnosis can make people feel very present with an experience, and for some people that is exactly the wrong thing early on. I focus on the first stage and on the present, not on digging for the past.

If trauma has left your body stuck on alert, building steady calm is a sensible first stage.

What Hypnosis Can Usefully Do

In the stabilisation stage, hypnotic techniques can help you build a reliable sense of calm and safety that you can access on demand, reduce the physical hyperarousal that keeps the body on alert, and ease sleep disturbance and nightmares for some people.

We can also work on the present-day consequences of trauma, such as panic, poor sleep, and self-blame, without needing to relive the event in detail. This is the approach I prefer: present-focused, paced, and always within your tolerance.

Important: Hypnosis is not a reliable way to recover memories. Memories retrieved under hypnosis can be inaccurate or even constructed. I do not use hypnosis to uncover or verify past events.

How I Pace the Work

A useful concept is the window of tolerance, a term from the psychiatrist Dan Siegel. It describes the zone in which you can feel and think without being flooded or shut down. Trauma tends to narrow it. Good trauma work keeps you within the window and gradually widens it.

In practice that means we go slowly, check in often, and prioritise resources such as calming imagery, grounding in the present, and a clear sense that you can stop at any time. We do not push towards intense material. If something comes up that is beyond the scope of our work, we pause and I will talk to you about how best to get you the right support.

Signs Trauma May Be Affecting Your Day-to-Day

Not all trauma looks dramatic. It can show up as being easily startled, a feeling of being on guard in situations that are objectively safe, avoiding certain places or topics, disrupted sleep, emotional numbness, irritability, or persistent shame.

If these sound familiar, they are worth taking seriously. They are common, understandable responses to overwhelming experience, and there is effective help available.

Who Should Be Cautious

If you have complex trauma, dissociation, current abuse or danger, severe flashbacks, or thoughts of harming yourself, you need a trauma-trained clinician involved. I will be honest if a case is beyond what I should be working on alone, and I am happy to coordinate with your therapist.

You remain in control throughout, and we can stop or change direction at any time.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can hypnotherapy treat PTSD?

It is not a first-line treatment. NICE recommends trauma-focused CBT and EMDR. Hypnotherapy may support stabilisation, calm, and sleep alongside those treatments.

Will I have to talk about what happened?

Not in detail. I prefer a present-focused approach that works on the consequences of trauma without requiring you to relive it.

Can hypnosis recover repressed memories?

No reliable evidence supports this, and memories produced under hypnosis can be unreliable. I do not use hypnosis for memory recovery.

Is it safe if I have flashbacks or dissociation?

It needs care. If these are significant, I would want a trauma-trained clinician involved, and we would go slowly.

Can I combine this with EMDR or trauma therapy?

Often yes, and with your therapist's agreement. It is sensible to tell them you are doing it.
